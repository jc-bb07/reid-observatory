The stagnation story: All three islands are aging rapidly, but their population trajectories differ. Jersey continues modest growth. IoM and Guernsey have effectively plateaued — population in 2021 is barely above 1991 levels once you account for the composition. The implication: dependency ratio pressure cannot be relieved by population growth alone.

What is a dependency ratio? It measures how many retired people exist for every 100 working-age people. A higher number means more pressure on public finances. IoM and Guernsey now exceed the UK; Jersey remains below it — but is closing the gap fast.

Reading the pyramids: A healthy growing population looks like a triangle — wide at the bottom. The UK shows a barrel shape — bulging in the middle. The Crown Dependencies tell a different story: a Grecian urn.

The base is narrow because young people leave for university and don't always return. The waist pinches at working age. Then it widens again in the 50s and 60s — that's the finance-sector cohort who arrived in the 1990s and early 2000s, now ageing in place.

This isn't about current immigration. It's the echo of decisions made 20–30 years ago, now showing up as a structural dependency problem. In side-by-side mode, the centre panel shows the difference between the two selected populations per age band.

IoM's 2026/2031/2041 years are different from the others: no official Isle of Man population projection exists (confirmed independently against the Statistics IoM Population Report 2025 and the Government's own housing-need evidence base). Instead of one invented number, the IoM scenario selector above lets you compare three outcomes built on the same real model: cohort-transition rates derived directly from the Island's own real census pyramids at 1996, 2001, 2011 and 2021 (comparing the real population in each age band at one census against the real population one band older at the next census). These rates are net of both mortality and migration exactly as the Island experienced them — a census snapshot can't cleanly separate the two.

Historical trend uses those rates as-is. Recent trend adds the confirmed 2022-2024 average net migration (600/yr) on top — and lands within a few hundred people of the Government's own official OAHN housing-need baseline (94,000 by 2041), an independent cross-check rather than a fitted match. Target back-solves the migration rate needed to hit the "Our Island, Our Future" Economic Strategy's own ambition of 100,000 by 2037 (~1,087/yr, nearly double the recent confirmed rate) — shown as the stated policy goal, not a forecast; it was a contested policy at the time, and government's own 2024 evidence base already concluded it's off track.

One disclosed simplification: the male/female split within each age band is held at its real 2021 ratio for all three future years, since no age/sex-specific migration or mortality data exists to project the split independently. This same cohort-transition model (not a separate methodology) also drives the population panel on the Isle of Man Government Pension Explorer.
The four groups: Pre-school (0–4), school-age (5–14), working-age (15–64), and retirement-age (65+). The last group is growing fastest. The working-age share — the people whose taxes fund public services — is shrinking across all three islands.

What happens next? Jersey, Guernsey and UK projections are from official published sources. The Isle of Man has no official projection at all, so instead of picking one number, this page shows four scenarios built on IoM's own real 1996-2021 census cohort behaviour: a Historical trend (no migration adjustment), the Government Actuary's own Actuarial Assumptions (300/yr, from the National Insurance Fund's 2023-GD-0005 review), a Recent trend (confirmed 2022-2024 migration continuing), and the Government's own stated Target of 100,000 people by 2037.

A real result, not an assumption: the higher-migration scenarios show a lower dependency ratio than the historical trend, not a higher one — because IoM's migrants arrive overwhelmingly working-age (25-49), which grows the workforce faster than the retired population grows. That's the actual mechanism the Government's own strategy is relying on. It also means the "no migration" historical case is the one with the worse dependency ratio outlook, not the reassuring one.

The 100,000 target: from the "Our Island, Our Future" Economic Strategy, approved by Tynwald in November 2022 — an explicit ambition (~1,000 net inward migrants/yr for 15 years), not a demographic forecast, and one that proved contested at the time. Government's own 2024 Objective Assessment of Housing Need (OAHN) evidence base already concluded it's unlikely to be met on current trends, with a baseline of around 94,000 by 2041 — which lines up closely with this page's own "Recent trend" scenario (93,558 by 2041), built independently from real cohort data rather than fitted to match.
