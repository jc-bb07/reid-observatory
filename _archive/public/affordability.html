Housing Affordability — Isle of Man

The median wage on the Isle of Man is £755 a week — marginally above the UK. On paper, that sounds adequate. In practice, buying a home on that income requires a mortgage of more than nine times annual earnings (9.09×). That is above the UK's already-stretched 8.2×, and more than double the generally accepted affordability threshold of 4×.

This page shows what housing actually costs relative to what people earn, what that leaves for everything else, and how the island compares with Jersey, Guernsey, and the UK. None of it makes comfortable reading.

Price-to-income ratios. House prices divided by annual earnings (weekly × 52.18). Two ratios shown: price ÷ mean annual wage and price ÷ median annual wage. The median ratio is the more policy-relevant figure — it captures the position of the typical worker, not the average distorted by high earners. Ratios above 4× are generally considered unaffordable by international housing standards (Demographia). Ratios above 8× are classified as "severely unaffordable".

Below living wage. Isle of Man Earnings Survey Report 2025 (Statistics IoM, Table 12, MIS methodology). 27.4% of IoM employees earn below the Living Wage in 2025, up from a confirmed 20.1% in 2024 — though a meaningful part of that jump reflects the Living Wage Foundation's own recalibration of the benchmark, not purely falling pay. The Living Wage is itself a minimum adequacy standard — falling below it means earnings are insufficient for a basic basket of goods and services.

Expenditure from MIS basket (IoM Living Wage Report 2024/25) with actual market-rate housing costs. IoM house prices vs median wage — over time
House prices on the Isle of Man are up around 57% in cash terms since 2009 (£228k to £358k). Wages have risen too, but inflation has eaten most of that gain — in real terms, pay has barely moved. What hasn't moved is the price-to-earnings ratio itself: 9.69× in 2009, still 9.09× today. That ratio is the more direct measure of the housing squeeze than either series on its own — and it shows a generation for whom ownership is increasingly out of reach regardless of how carefully they save.
Data. IoM house prices: Land Registry annual averages (Housing Market Review 2025, confirmed full-year figure for 2025; prior years from Housing Market Review 2023). IoM median wage: Annual Earnings Survey, June (2025 figure from Earnings Survey Report 2025). Both indexed 2009=100 for comparability. Both series are nominal — this chart shows cash-terms movement, not the affordability ratio itself (see the price-to-median-earnings ratio above, which is the more directly comparable measure of the affordability squeeze).

What's left after the bills — three household profiles
These three profiles show what happens to median-wage income once housing, utilities, food, transport, and basic living costs are paid. The spending figures are drawn from the Minimum Income Standard — the IoM Government's estimate of what households need for an adequate standard of living. We think the MIS understates real costs, so treat these as optimistic. For many households the reality is tighter still.
Model. IoM 2024/25 tax rates applied precisely (PA £14,500; 10% band £6,500; 20% above; NI 11% on £8,736–£53,664; 1% above). Expenditure: MIS basket (IoM Living Wage Report 2024/25). Housing: market-rate private rent (1-bed £750/month; 3-bed £1,450/month). Utilities: Manx Utilities/Manx Gas 2024 actuals. IoM domestic rates excluded for renters (landlord's liability). Child Benefit included where applicable (£25.60/wk, 2024/25). This is an indicative model — the MIS basket is a floor, not an average. Actual household expenditure may differ significantly.
