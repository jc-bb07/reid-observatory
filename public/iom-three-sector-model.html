The Three-Sector Hypothesis — CLDs & first evidence

Isle of Man system-dynamics economic model · working page v1 · built from the observatory data-library, 18 Jul 2026
“Our competition isn’t with the globe. It’s internal: the public sector, the existing private sector, and whichever sector is this year’s flavour of the month, all competing for the same scarce housing, people and capital.”

1 · The headline test

If the Island were competing with the globe, decades of successive promoted sectors (finance liberalisation → film → space → e-gaming → fintech/digital) should have compounded into growth. If the competition is internal, promoted sectors should reallocate a roughly fixed factor base instead. The factor base is indeed roughly fixed — and total real output has barely moved.

2 · Causal loop diagrams

Three linked views of one structure. Arrows carry polarity (+ same direction, − opposite); R marks reinforcing loops, B balancing loops; ∥ marks a significant delay.

CLD 1 — The promotion engine (“flavour of the month”)

Policy attention grows the promoted sector, which flatters headline GVA and rewards the policy (R1). But the same growth bids up scarce staff and premises, squeezing the existing private sector (B1), and the sector itself matures under global competition (∥), so attention rotates to the next flavour — the loop restarts with a new protagonist rather than compounding.

CLD 2 — The fixed factor pool

Population growth is migration-only, and migration is throttled by housing costs (B2); housing supply responds slowly (B3, ∥). So labour is quasi-fixed and the three sectors bid against each other for it (B4).

CLD 3 — The public-sector asymmetry

The public sector competes for people and housing but not for customers: its revenue comes from taxing the other two sectors. Its headcount growth is a one-way ratchet (∥) that tightens the factor pool (B5) until the tax base itself erodes (B6, long delay via reserves).

CLD 4 — The revenue engine: Common Purse → FERSA (added on James’s identification of the VAT loop)

What funded the public-sector ratchet without taxing the other two sectors: under the 1979 revenue-sharing agreement, more on-island spending meant a bigger measured share of pooled UK+IoM VAT — spend more, receive more (R2). The loop ran hot through the 2000s until the UK rebased the share in 2009 and again in 2011 (exogenous amputation). Since 2016, FERSA throttles it: an expenditure survey every ~5 years resets the share, indexation fills the gaps, and a retrospective straight-line true-up corrects past years (B7) — which is why the last Treasury team quietly set money aside, and why a rebalancing payment to HM Treasury duly followed in February 2025.

F · The engine, running and amputated: Customs & Excise receipts, 1998/99–2023/24

The Island’s share of pooled VAT & duties nearly tripled in eight years — £156m (1998/99) to £438m (2006/07), rising from 52% to 72% of all Treasury receipts while resident income tax fell from 38% to 25%. This is R2 running free: the public-spending decade was funded by an external transfer, not by taxing the Island’s own economy — which is why the crowding-out cost stayed invisible and the finance sector could take the flak. The UK’s 2009 and 2011 rebasings then cut receipts by a third by 2012/13; spending was defended anyway (the ratchet), via reserves. FERSA (2016) rebuilt the line under survey-and-true-up discipline.

Sources: Digest of Economic & Social Statistics 2009, tables 16.5–16.6; Isle of Man in Numbers 2017, table 10; light-blue-book extracts (iom_finances_wide.csv) for 2014/15–2023/24. 4 · Reading — does the theory survive contact with the data?

Supported. The factor base is quasi-fixed exactly as the theory requires (population +0.3% in 13 years, employment flat, natural change negative). Successive promoted sectors reallocated shares rather than compounding growth: total real GVA rose 10% in a quarter-century in which the UK — the natural control for global shocks — rose 54%. The crowding-out signature is visible in real levels, not just shares: manufacturing −47%, agriculture −38%, retail −25%, finance −9%, while the winners were the promoted sector, construction, rentals and the public sector. House prices rose 77% (2005–25) with almost no population growth.

One refinement the data forces. The three competitors are not symmetric. The current flavour (e-gaming) is employment-light — ~1,100 jobs producing ~15–20% of GVA — so it competes fiercely in the statistics but only modestly for people. The heavyweight in the internal labour and housing competition is the public sector (+2,643 jobs 2001–21, real public-admin GVA +56%), and its bidding is funded by taxing the other two. The model should therefore treat the public sector as the slow, ratcheting competitor, the promoted sector as the fast, GVA-flattering one, and the existing private sector as the residual claimant on whatever housing and people remain.

The funding mechanism (James’s VAT-loop point, confirmed). The public-sector ratchet of the 2000s was financed by the Common Purse reinforcing loop, not by domestic taxation — C&E receipts tripled 1998/99–2006/07 while the income-tax share of revenue shrank. The timing matches the real economy exactly: public-admin real GVA grew ~31% in 1998–2008 (loop running), went flat 2008–2018 (post-rebasing squeeze), and headcount never fell when the funding was cut — expansion on external money, defence on internal money. Under FERSA the loop survives in throttled form: measured on-island expenditure still feeds the share, but a ~5-yearly survey with straight-line retrospective true-up now claws back over-payments — hence Treasury “prudently” holding funds for five years and the February 2025 rebalancing payment to HMT. The political-economy corollary: each flavour-of-the-month narrative supplied the growth story that justified a spending level the VAT engine actually created.

Caveats for the write-up. (1) IoM real-terms series deflate finance with an economy-wide deflator — the −9% is sensitive to that choice. (2) ESA10 methodology break at 2007/08; pre/post shares are not strictly comparable. (3) Median transaction price is not mix-adjusted. (4) Census sector definitions shift over time (source’s own warning). (5) UK is an imperfect control — it had its own policy dysfunctions; Jersey/Guernsey comparators (in the library) would strengthen the decomposition. None of these plausibly reverse the sign of the findings.

5 · The 2011 paper: Repositioning “Offshore” — the fifteen-year scorecard

The theory on this page is not new. It was delivered to Treasury in 2011 (J. Cochrane, Repositioning “Offshore”, with the supporting systems-dynamics dissertation of 2009/10), including the causal loop diagrams, the UK-differencing method used in the charts above, and the VAT loop — “IOM Government spending fuelled economic growth, which increased the IOM proportion of the revenue received from the UK/IOM VAT agreement, which fuelled IOM Government spending”. Everything since is out-of-sample. The scorecard:

CONFIRMED — “Indicators suggest that this limit to growth was reached sometime between 1994 and 2002… active management of the IOM economy is futile, at best.”
Real GVA +10% over 1998–2022 vs UK +54%; population flat 2011–24; total employment lower in 2021 than 2001. The Island has been at its factor ceiling throughout.
CONFIRMED — “If one industry is comparatively incentivised, it will price out other industries” — tested, the paper insisted, by “adjusting for UK industrial growth”.
e-Gaming & ICT +11.8pp of GVA share 2007–23 while manufacturing real output fell 47%, retail 25%, finance 9%. Charts A and B above use exactly the 2010 method.
CONFIRMED & EXTENDED — The VAT “vicious cycle… effectively curbed with the UK-enforced, September 2009 renegotiation.”
C&E receipts £156m → £438m (1998/99–2006/07), then cut to £296m by 2012/13 (chart F). FERSA (2016) formalised the throttle with survey-and-true-up; Treasury provisioned for the clawback and paid the rebalancing in February 2025.
CONFIRMED — “Manx Government over-spend… increases the General Price Level on the Island due to supply-side contention; pushing out private sector business.”
Public sector +2,643 jobs 2001–21 in a labour market that shrank; median house price +77% (2005–25) on near-zero population growth.
CONFIRMED (directionally) — “Industrial targeting… leads to a forced emigration of young Manx people in favour of immigration of experienced individuals in the target industry.”
Natural change negative every year 2017–24 (−351 in 2024); all growth is inward migration (~650/yr). The age structure is doing what the 2011 mechanism said it would.
CONFIRMED — 14 years later — “The international response (multi-lateral tax code simplification) will take some time, although it must ultimately succeed.”
OECD Pillar Two 15% global minimum tax: the Isle of Man adopted the income-inclusion rule and a domestic top-up tax for periods from 1 January 2025. The 0% CIT perimeter was breached exactly as forecast — by external policy, on external timing.
NOT (YET) BORNE OUT — “Localised economic crash is certain if current policy continues… the price differential will govern the length of following local recession.”
No crash; instead two decades of relative stagnation. Two cushions the 2011 paper could see only dimly: the e-gaming rotation (a fresh “ephemeral” demand source — which the promotion engine, R1, itself predicts the system will find) and the FERSA-era rebuild of the VAT line. The predicted trigger — the end of ephemeral demand via international policy — only fully arrived with Pillar Two in 2025. This row is the one to watch, not the one to retire.
CONFIRMED — including the order — (Dissertation, 2010): industries “collapse in sequence — beginning with those industries most exposed to substitution — e.g. retail and tourism.”
Census employment 2001–21: Retail & wholesale −1,314, Manufacturing −1,135, Primary −1,027, Hospitality −814 — the substitutable sectors fell first and hardest, exactly in the exposure order the archetype predicted, while the captive sector (public) grew +2,643. The Tragedy-of-the-Commons sequencing was a genuine forecast: chart C is its verification, eleven years later.
Provenance note. The 2009/10 dissertation modelled the Island as four sectors — Non-Tradable, Substitutable (tradable, price-capped by the world), “Ephemeral Services” (demand driven by international tax incidence), and the Public Sector. Today’s three competitors are the direct descendants: the public sector, the existing private sector (non-tradable + substitutable), and the flavour of the month (ephemeral). The dissertation’s summary model “shows the two industries and Government sharing resources of the wider economy. The constraint is depicted as labour — constrained land would perhaps be more accurate” — i.e., the three-sector hypothesis, stated in 2010. It also supplies calibration constants for the simulator: ~6–7 years from capacity change to inflation; ~3–4 years from peak emigration to trough house prices. The figures appendix of the 2011 award submission already contained an “Incomplete Causal Loop Diagram of the Public Sector” and panel charts of IoM industry growth adjusted for the UK — the diagrams on this page are second drafts, fifteen years on.
