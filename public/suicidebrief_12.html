The island suicide rate
has a critical data problem.

These last few years, the Isle of Man's suicide rate has felt personally alarming. Friends, friends-of-friends, people you know — in a community this size, every loss is felt. You don't just let that go, not about home, and you shouldn't. So, as is my way, I went looking wider: at Jersey, at Guernsey, at the Scottish islands and the rest. And the pattern that is easily assumed was ours alone turned out to run wider than the Isle of Man. Had we found an Island problem?

This piece is not saying these deaths matter less. Every one of them is reviewed individually, and everything that follows is about pushing harder on what actually helps — making sure the people who most need help get the right treatment in time, backing local peer support, tackling the loneliness and exclusion behind so many of these deaths. Small close-knit communities, isolation, service access, stigma. Different islands, different years, the same story every time. So is it real?

What the analysis found

1. No island stands out, in either direction

The method was written down and registered publicly before I saw any answer, then run across eleven territories from 2001 to 2024 and cross-checked in a second statistical package. The fair test is not each island against "the UK" as a whole, but against the spread of comparable-size places — the Crown Dependencies, Isle of Wight and Anglesey set among England and Wales's local authorities, the Scottish islands among Scotland's council areas — with the national rates (England and Wales around 11, Scotland around 14) as the benchmark. Measured that way, and allowing for how little a handful of deaths can pin down, no island stands out. The Isle of Man sits on the higher side of that ordinary range, but not as an outlier — and at these numbers, not in any way that can be told apart from ordinary variation. Jersey sits below its benchmark; the rest fall in the middle. The deeper reason it can go no further is simple: these deaths are, thankfully, rare enough that a place the size of the Isle of Man cannot produce a rate precise enough to settle the question either way. So the sensible move is to lean on the standard evidence base for suicide prevention rather than keep hunting a distinctly island cause, and to revisit only if several years of honestly published counts run consistently above the benchmark, not on the strength of any single year.

2. So why does every island look like an emergency?

Because of how the number is built, in three separate ways. The headline rate is not a count of deaths. First, it is a rolling average — three years for the Crown Dependencies, five for the Scottish islands — the standard way to steady a rare figure in a small population, and none of this is the islands' fault. But put one extraordinary year through that smoother and it does not stay one year. Second, turning a handful of deaths into a rate per 100,000 is itself a magnifier on a population this small: on the Isle of Man, one extra death moves the rate by about 1.2 points; on Shetland, with under a third of the people, one death moves it by more than 4. Third, a death is counted in the year its inquest concludes, not the year it happened — so a backlog can pile several years' deaths into a single one, inflating it before the averaging even starts. (The full analysis charts every island's rate.)

The upshot. Much of the "record" is built, not lived. By date of death 2020 was a genuinely bad year — nineteen deaths — but the published twenty-four is inflated by inquest timing, and a three-year average then smears that spike across its neighbours, so the line crests in 2019, before the event that caused it. As that spike ages out of the rolling window, the "record" rate falls back on its own. We read nothing into the surrounding years: at these numbers a swing of two or three deaths is ordinary chance, not a trend.

3. It works in reverse, too

The same smoother manufactures good news as readily as bad. Shetland's rate looks like a flat four-year success story. Its decline is real, raw deaths there roughly halved. But the flat floor in the published chart is largely two ordinary quiet years counted four times over.

Same exercise, five-year window this time. Watch 2016 and 2017, both of which registered zero deaths, land inside four separate published figures at once. Watch, too, how flat the raw deaths-per-year line looks, next to how volatile that same line becomes once it's turned into a rate.

Zero is not an error. Shetland has around 23,000 people. Two quiet years running is a routine outcome at that size, not a miracle and not a mistake. Nothing here says the decline is not real. What the flat floor adds is a shape the raw counts do not support: four published points built from the same two years.

What this does not say. It does not say the numbers are small so it does not matter. Every one of these deaths warrants review, and the risks named in the evidence are real: the Island's own suicide audit found a third of the sixty-five people who died between 2016 and 2021 were unemployed or unable to work; debt, loneliness, living alone, long-term disability and relationship breakdown run through the record; a separate audit of mid-life men who died found many had previously self-harmed or lived with a serious physical illness; and adults who need mental-health care still cannot reliably be seen in time. None of that needs a rate to justify acting on it, and nothing here should delay that support by a single day.
What should change
  • Publish honest annual counts, as National Records of Scotland already does, instead of a rolling headline that turns one year into a trend.
  • Show the uncertainty on any smoothed figure that is published, as the ONS does.
  • Make sure the people who most need help are seen in time — and publish whether they are, as Jersey and Scotland do — alongside who leads, who commissions and who delivers each part of the Strategy. No data reform matters more.
  • Compare islands to islands, on the same ruler. Holding 85,000 people against 60 million mostly measures the arithmetic, not the health service — and Crown Dependencies, Scottish islands and England & Wales aren't even measured the same way.
  • Act on the risk factors already known — unemployment and financial exclusion, relationship breakdown, prior self-harm, physical illness, alcohol — rather than waiting on a rate that cannot settle anything.

No island is the outlier here. The ruler is.

Handled carelessly, statistics frighten communities, stigmatise islands, and send scarce help chasing a problem that may not be there, while every real death still matters just as much. While the honest numbers turn out to be kinder than the smoothed ones, it remains just as urgent to act on the risks that are real — the waits, the welfare, the work.

Small island after small island. Different economies, different geographies, hundreds of miles apart, each with a line that looks alarming, at its own time and for its own reasons. That was the pattern. The only honest thing to do was to stop looking and test whether any of it was real.

Act IIIThe test

Before I could talk myself into an answer, I wrote the study down and registered it publicly (OSF), so the method was fixed before I saw the result. The registered question was the sharpest version of my own theory of why islands might differ: within each territory, do suicide deaths rise and fall with labour-market exclusion, once the shocks every territory shares are stripped out? And beneath it, the question everyone actually asks: are the islands' rates genuinely higher than Great Britain's at all?

I built a panel from 2001 to 2024: the small islands, with England & Wales, Scotland and Prince Edward Island as comparison anchors. (I wanted the Faroe Islands in too, but they keep no comparable annual series; Gotland scraped in with only three usable years: the harmonisation problem, before I had even begun.) I benchmarked correctly, because there is no single "UK" rate to beat. England & Wales sits near 11, Scotland near 14. Then I tested it several ways: a fixed-effects Poisson model with a wild cluster bootstrap; empirical-Bayes shrinkage; the pre-registered panel; a synthetic control for Shetland with placebo inference; and a year-by-year analysis of the Isle of Man. I cross-checked the whole thing in a second statistical engine (R).

Here is what stopped me. Take the level question first: are the islands' rates genuinely higher? The fair test sets each island not against "the UK" but against the spread of comparable places its own size — the Scottish islands within Scotland's thirty-two council areas, and the Crown Dependencies, Isle of Wight and Anglesey within the local authorities of England and Wales. Shrink each rate for how little a handful of deaths can pin down, and no island stands out. The Isle of Man leans highest, at roughly four-to-one odds its true rate is above the England and Wales average, yet it lands around the eightieth percentile of English and Welsh local authorities: a place near the top of an ordinary spread, not an outlier. Jersey sits below its comparator; Orkney and Na h-Eileanan Siar lean high against Scotland in the same inconclusive way; the rest cluster in the middle. My own theory fared no better: the pre-registered panel, testing whether suicide rises and falls with labour-market exclusion within each territory once shared shocks are stripped out, came back a clean null. And why the level question had to be settled by shrinkage, and the theory by a pooled panel, is the point that matters most: any single island, on its own, is far too sparse to detect even a large difference. The Isle of Man alone is sixteen years of data and a single cluster; its headline rate cannot settle the question in either direction.

That is the data problem in one line: the number everyone quotes is the one number that can never answer it.

A note on Guernsey. It publishes only 3-year smoothed counts, so it could not enter the pre-registered panel at all; its verdict here rests on the direct comparison of rates and confidence intervals. That it is the hardest island to test in the first place is the data problem in miniature.

Public Health had, in fairness, made the first half of this point to the committee scrutinising all this: Tynwald's Social Affairs Policy Review Committee, whose 684-page interim report on suicide runs through the rest of this piece. At these numbers, Public Health told it, the rate cannot demonstrate a statistically significant difference; it is "not very sensitive."

"A suicide rate is still, thankfully, a rare event. So in order to demonstrate a statistically significant difference, whether that is up or down, it needs a long time. So it is not very sensitive. … We use a three-year rolling average, which is something that is advised by the Office for National Statistics … In that time frame, it is very unlikely that we would say there is a statistically significant change either up or down in that short period of time."Dr Sean McLachlan, Public Health, oral evidence 16 June 2023 · SAPRC First Report 2025-26 (PP 2026/0133), pp. 233 & 235

A year later they went further than I would have dared to on their behalf:

"We cannot be looking at that headline rate of suicide because it is a rare event in a small population. There are natural peaks and troughs without those actually affecting the rate."Dr Sean McLachlan, Public Health, oral evidence 22 July 2024 · PP 2026/0133, p. 318

The committee asked, more than once, why the rate had climbed. The answer, each time, was that nobody knew, and that the search for a cause had come up empty. Because the question nobody put on the table is the one this piece began with: if there is no difference the rate can show, why does every island still look like an emergency?

Act IVThe artefact: a bad year, inflated into a record

I will be honest: being proved wrong stung. I gave myself a day, then went looking for what I had missed. It was hiding in how the number is built.

Two things are stacked on top of each other here. The headline is a 3-year or 5-year rolling average, the standard, ONS-recommended way to steady a rare event in a small population, where a handful of deaths and the timing of their registration would otherwise swing the figure around wildly. But put one hard year through that smoother and it does not stay one year: it smears backwards as well as forwards, which is why the lines in Act II crest in 2019, a year before the event that made them — a window centred on 2019 already contains 2020. Underneath the smoothing sits a second, independent magnifier: converting a small death count into a rate per 100,000 scales up whatever that count is. On the Isle of Man's ~85,000 people, one extra death moves the rate by about 1.2 points; on Shetland's ~23,000, by more than 4. Neither step is a trick, and both are standard practice — but stacked together, on a genuinely bad year, they turn a handful of deaths into a line that towers over a flat national comparator. None of this is the islands' fault.

Strip it back to the raw counts and the headline's real source shows. The published series counts deaths by year of registration — when the inquest concludes — and on that basis 2020 is a spike of 24. By year of death it was 19; a pandemic-era inquest backlog had registered the other five in 2020 though they occurred in other years. On any count, 2020 was a genuinely bad year. But the "record, 60% above the UK" is not that year speaking: it is the registration spike, inflated by backlog, taken by a three-year average and smeared across its neighbours. We do not read anything into the years on either side of it: at these numbers, a swing of two or three deaths is ordinary, and no single year — up or down — can be told apart from chance. Act VThe verdict: a glimmer of light
So, do the islands have a suicide problem?
No island is measurably worse off than Great Britain, and none measurably better; at these numbers the rate simply cannot tell.

They have a data problem.

The numbers are too sparse to rule out a modest difference either way. That's the point: the rate cannot answer the question it is being asked.

Not "small numbers, so it does not matter." Every death warrants review, exactly as Public Health argues.

The rate should inform, not alarm.

The fix is unglamorous: publish honest annual counts, as National Records of Scotland already does; present any smoothed figure with its uncertainty, as the ONS does.

Spend the energy on the risk factors this committee's own evidence already names.

Statistics can be dangerous.Handled carelessly, they frighten communities, stigmatise islands, and send scarce help chasing a problem that may not be there, while every real death still matters just as much.

None of this makes the suffering any less real. The evidence running through the committee's 684 pages names the real risks, and none of them is a rate: a third of the 65 people who died between 2016 and 2021 were unemployed or unable to work, on an Island whose registered unemployment is close to zero; Public Health adds debt, loneliness, living alone, long-term disability; the Chief Constable adds marital breakdown; the then Health Minister adds poverty itself. Behind it all sits an adult mental-health waiting list that, by the committee's own admission, still has not moved. Those are not artefacts. They need no rate to justify acting on them, and nothing in this analysis should delay that support by a single day.

I hate being wrong about my own hypothesis. But there is a glimmer of light in this one: if the null holds, being wrong was worth it. These islands can stop chasing a difference the numbers were never able to show, record the real figures plainly, with caveats, and put scarce help where it truly saves lives: the waits, the welfare, the work. The honest numbers turn out to be kinder than the smoothed ones.

Island after island, in small communities the world over, the picture is the same: no place is the tragic outlier its rolling rate, and its headlines, have made it seem.

Policy recommendations: what to do instead

The committee's 684 pages, careful as they are, stop at eleven conclusions and make no formal recommendations; its members are explicit that they are "not experts". These are offered in that spirit, as concrete next steps for others to test and improve.

Redirect the effort to action
Stop explaining a difference the rate can't show
Stop hunting a special island cause
"Why is our rate higher?" cannot be answered from the rate, not for want of effort, but because the numbers cannot carry it. The committee asked it, more than once, and Public Health's honest answer was that nobody knows. That inconclusive search is not a failure. It is the correct answer, arriving. The 684-page interim report it produced is roughly ninety pages of analysis atop nearly six hundred of annexes, transcripts and attached policies. Put that effort into case-level review of every death, and into the actions below. Lean into the standard evidence base and support that already works elsewhere, rather than continuing to search for a distinctly island cause — a search worth reopening only if several years of honestly published counts run consistently above the comparator, not on the strength of any single year.
Formalises the Committee's own evidence
What happened in Shetland?
Shetland's rate ran high through the 2000s, then fell after 2013 and has sat below the Scottish rate since — that part is real: raw annual deaths roughly halved, from about 5.25/yr before 2013 to about 2/yr after. But the published shape — a sharp drop to ~7, then a flat floor there for four straight points (2015-2018) — overstates itself. Two ordinary years, 2016 and 2017, both registered zero deaths, and sit inside all four of those windows at once (see Act IV). Shetland isn't magical; it's small, and small populations have 0-years from time to time. Did sustained local prevention work cause the underlying decline? No one can prove that either way at this scale. But don't reach for the flat trough as if it were four years of independent confirmation — it's largely two years, counted four times.
Act, don't wait for proof
Monitor what's sensitive, not what's blunt
Public Health itself asked for richer measures than the headline rate: a "suicide prevention profile," and the reach of interventions (people trained, people supported, waits shortened). Track those. They change in months, not the decade the rate needs.
Public Health's own ask
Publish the map of who leads, who commissions, who delivers
In February 2024 the committee recognised "a frustration with the pace of change, a perception of a lack of clear leadership for the Strategy and Government's apparent inability to work more closely with the third sector", and by 2026 it recorded that engagement had improved. Public Health is the Strategy's strategic lead: it coordinates implementation, monitors progress, holds partner agencies to account, and has itself delivered the Suspected Suicide Rapid Response group, suicide audit and intelligence work, postvention support and QPR training. What it is not, and could not reasonably be, is the operational deliverer of every action: it neither runs nor holds the budget for Manx Care's clinical mental-health services, so actions needing clinical staffing, capacity or crisis pathways sit with Manx Care as provider and DHSC as commissioner. The gap the committee's evidence points to is not an absent owner but an unpublished map of that division. Publish it.
Governance
Do what works, whether or not any island differs
Prevention that stands on its own
Measure labour-market exclusion properly, then act on it
It is the strongest risk signal in the whole picture, and on the Isle of Man it hides behind the Island's proudest statistic. Registered unemployment is close to zero: "hardly anyone unemployed," the Chief Constable told the committee, against 746 Jobcentre vacancies. Yet the Island's own audit (GD2023/0019) found a third of those who died were unemployed or unable to work, a 4.1x overrepresentation — well above the roughly 2.5x higher odds of suicide the wider international literature finds among unemployed people generally, which suggests the local signal is not an artefact of definitions alone. Both figures are true at once, because the headline number counts people signing on, not the economically inactive, the long-term sick, the "unable to work", which is where the risk actually lives. The Government's own numbers show the gap: its broader, ILO-style estimate has run 1.6–2.4 times the published claimant-count rate in recent years (2.8% vs 1.8%, December 2020; 1.7% vs 0.7%, March 2025), and in March 2025 nearly six times as many people were registered sick while claiming Income Support (1,898) as were registered unemployed (322). The measured rate undercuts the real rate of exclusion: the same curse as the suicide rate, wearing a different statistic. And across the islands it is measured inconsistently anyway (JSA here, claimant count there, different denominators everywhere), so it cannot even be compared. Measure exclusion on a common basis, publish it beside the mortality data, and treat employment and financial security as frontline suicide prevention. (Our own territory-level panel finds no detectable co-movement once shared shocks are stripped out; that is an ecological result, and it neither tests nor overturns the individual-level audit signal, which stands.)

Treat separation and relationship breakdown as a crisis window
Marital breakdown is already in the committee's evidence, named by the Chief Constable among the risks running through the cases. The wider literature gives that a sharper edge: separated men die by suicide at several times the rate of married men, with risk concentrated in the weeks immediately after separation, and meta-analyses put the odds of suicidal ideation, attempts or completed suicide at roughly 1.6–2.8 times higher for the separated or divorced than the married. That is a short, identifiable window, and one the Island already has contact points for: family courts, GPs, and relationship-support and advice services all see people at the point of separation, before any crisis service does. A simple, time-limited signposting step at each of those points — in the weeks after separation, not the months — costs little and targets exactly where the risk concentrates.

Fix the adult mental-health wait — and start by publishing it
The committee's conclusion is blunt: the new therapists, hubs and practitioners have "not had an impact on waiting lists for adults yet". The children's side has at least moved — the CAMHS waiting list fell from 1,205 in April 2024 to 905 a year later, and a new assessment service launched in 2025 — but the adult wait has not. And here is the deeper problem: there is no published adult mental-health waiting time to hold anyone to. Manx Care publishes crisis-response, psychosis-assessment and discharge-follow-up standards, and meets them; but on the routine adult wait, the DHSC's own assessment of Manx Care says only that the data "is currently being developed to improve its accuracy". Everyone else publishes one: Jersey an assessment-within-10-working-days standard (94% in 2025), England its Talking Therapies 6- and 18-week access, and Public Health Scotland an 18-week psychological-therapies standard for every board — the island boards of Orkney, Shetland and Na h-Eileanan Siar included. The Isle of Man and Guernsey are the only two islands here that publish no adult waiting time at all. A caution travels with the metric, though: a waiting time is a guide, not a verdict on need — a long list can reflect referral patterns and where the triage line is drawn as much as genuine unmet demand, and what finally matters is not list length but whether the people who most need care get the right treatment in time. That is the measure worth publishing: a priority-based one, of the kind Manx Care already applies to its urgent cases (a first episode of psychosis seen within two weeks, a crisis referral within the hour), extended to routine adult access. You cannot fix, or even honestly debate, a wait you will not measure. Publish one that means something.

Recognise peer support, and build any bridge to services with care
Public Health works with a wide range of suicide-prevention, bereavement, mental-health and community organisations, and produced a formal multi-agency response after the ManxPACT report in January 2026. A number of peer-led groups across the Island — among them Ed Space's men's and women's evenings, ManxPACT's Saturday meet-ups and Women's Talk — provide valued, accessible support, and may reach people who don't readily approach statutory services. Recognising that value is not the same as commissioning it: the committee's evidence supports only the general principle that a community facility "could have links to statutory service provision, with the ability to signpost or refer those in need," not an endorsement of any named provider or referral model. Where a group's referral-based proposal did not proceed — cancelled from presentation to the Manx Care Board, with DHSC declining to support GP referrals into a group it "did not know about" — those are decisions for the Manx Care Board and DHSC to account for, not a verdict on Public Health. Any formal link should be developed collaboratively, with proportionate governance, safeguarding, evaluation and agreed escalation pathways, alongside treatment — never as a way to move people off a waiting list instead of treating them.

Follow up on prior self-harm, physical illness and alcohol, not just diagnosis
The Island's own mid-life-men audit found that 44% of those who died had previously self-harmed — 7% of them in the week before death — and that more than half had a significant physical health condition: both stronger local signals than the headline rate can ever be. Nationally, alcohol runs through a large share of suicides too: present at post-mortem in roughly a fifth to two-thirds of cases depending on the series, with alcohol use disorder alone associated with a roughly five- to six-fold increase in risk. None of this needs a rate to justify acting on it: systematic follow-up after every self-harm presentation, closer working between physical-health teams and mental-health services, and routine alcohol screening in primary and emergency care are all deliverable now, on the evidence already in hand.

Publish more, by occurrence-year — safely, and separately from SSRR
Strategy action 2.3.2, the Suspected Suicide Rapid Response (SSRR) dataset, was marked complete in September 2025, "populated by Health Intelligence analyst live during SSRR meetings". SSRR is, rightly, a confidential operational tool for rapid response, safeguarding, postvention and cluster identification: it can hold live, unverified information, including about people still living, and it should stay that way. The ask here is narrower and separate: additional aggregate statistics by year of occurrence, drawn from data once it clears coronial validation, published on a regular schedule. Public Health already publishes suicide statistics through the Isle of Man Mortality Reports; this would extend that record, not replace it, and any change needs coronial input, disclosure control and engagement with bereaved families and people with lived experience before it moves. Scotland's ScotSID is a useful reference for how a secure, person-level linked dataset can feed aggregate statistical reporting, subject to the same legal and governance tests here — not a case for publishing person-level records, in Scotland or here. And "not collected" overstates the attempted-suicide gap: individual services may hold relevant information, but Public Health has no consolidated Island-wide dataset for population-level surveillance of attempted suicide.

Publish annual counts. Retire the rolling headline
National Records of Scotland already publishes annual council-area counts, including years of 0, 1 or 2. And the ask is in the committee's own evidence: the Samaritans branch director, wishing for statistics ("Is the trend up or down? … We do not see those stats"), was asked by a committee member whether suicide should be reported like road deaths, published annually, and answered "I do not see why not." Honest annual counts, presented with care, are more transparent than 3-year smoothing, not less safe. The rolling-average headline is what turns one year into a trend. If a single smoothed figure is genuinely needed, use one that carries its uncertainty, an empirical-Bayes estimate or an annual count with its confidence interval, not a moving average, which manufactures trends out of noise.

Harmonise measurement across the islands
Crown Dependencies use a 3-year window, the Scottish islands a 5-year one, England & Wales an annual one. Some count by registration, others differ (see the charts above, they are not even on the same ruler). A common basis is needed before any cross-island comparison means anything.
Comparability
Compare like with like: islands to islands
The Island's rate is habitually set against the UK. That has its uses, but for a rare event in a population of 85,000 it mostly measures the arithmetic, not the health system: you learn little by holding 85,000 people against 60 million. Small jurisdictions are better read against each other, or against scale-matched comparators, than against a national average. Scale is the malfunction, not the island.

Data governance & methodology

This is a pre-registered analysis of published, population-level statistics. It uses only territory-year aggregates, no individual is identifiable, and it follows Samaritans media guidelines throughout.

Sources. ONS suicide registrations (England & Wales); National Records of Scotland Probable Suicides 2024 for the Scottish council-area annual counts; Isle of Man Public Health Directorate mortality reports and correspondence, with year-of-death counts from a Constabulary FOI (ref 3968233); States of Guernsey Public Health (3-year averages only); Statistics Jersey / Health and Care Jersey; the SAPRC First Report 2025-26 (PP 2026/0133); and, as international comparators, Prince Edward Island (Statistics Canada) and Gotland (Socialstyrelsen). Labour-market figures: Isle of Man in Numbers (Cabinet Office, Economic Affairs), 2021 and 2025 editions.

Methods. A pre-registered fixed-effects Poisson panel (PPML) with a wild cluster bootstrap and CR3 small-cluster inference; empirical-Bayes shrinkage; a synthetic control for Shetland with placebo inference; and a year-by-year Isle of Man analysis. Cross-checked in a second engine (R, fixest). Published rolling rates are shown as each agency publishes them (3-year for the Crown Dependencies, 5-year for the Scottish islands, 2013 ESP). The unsmoothed charts use crude annual rates (deaths divided by population); these are not age-standardised, and are shown precisely to reveal the raw volatility a single death creates.

Honest limits. The numbers are small. For any single island the rate has almost no power to detect even a large difference; pooled across territories the study is adequately powered, and finds no association in either direction. The labour-market-exclusion exposure is itself a proxy — registered/claimant-count data, a narrower construct than a labour-force-survey unemployment or economic-inactivity rate would capture, and not designed to capture the "unable to work" population the Island's own audit flags as a predictor. The Government's own broader, ILO-style estimate has run 1.6–2.4 times the published claimant-count rate in recent years (2.8% vs 1.8%, December 2020; 1.7% vs 0.7%, March 2025; Isle of Man in Numbers, Cabinet Office), and classical measurement error of that kind biases a test like this toward finding nothing — so the null should be read with that possibility open, not as proof the exposure doesn't matter. Registration-year and occurrence-year counting differ. Nothing here licenses individual-level inference about who dies, or why.

The full work. The pre-registration is public: osf.io/nrf4s (OSF Registries, Secondary Data Preregistration, registered 29 July 2026).
