Small jurisdictions, outsized output

GDP and GVA per head, real growth rates, and output relative to the UK across the Isle of Man, Jersey, and Guernsey — 2000 to present. All three Crown Dependencies produce substantially more output per resident than the United Kingdom, though by different margins and for different structural reasons. Sources: IoM National Income reports; Statistics Jersey; States of Guernsey Statistics; ONS ABML/EBAQ/ABMI.

View
All three Crown Dependencies produce more output per person than the UK — significantly more. In 2023, the Isle of Man generated an estimated nearly twice as much per head as the UK in nominal terms, with Jersey and Guernsey broadly similar. But the headline number hides something important: these islands are financial centres. Much of their output is generated by banks, insurance companies, funds, and online businesses whose profits belong to shareholders and clients who often live elsewhere. A high GDP per head doesn't mean everyone on the island earns twice the UK average — it means the island hosts a lot of economic activity relative to its population.

Switch to Real (constant prices) to strip out inflation and see how purchasing power has actually changed over time. The Isle of Man's real output per head peaked around 2016/17 and has since declined — the nominal figure looks healthier because prices have risen, not because the economy has grown.
⚠ These figures measure output produced on the island, not income earned by residents. The gap between the two — particularly large in financial centres — means GDP/GVA per head is a poor proxy for average living standards or household wealth.

⚠ Don't mistake output for prosperity. High GDP or GVA per head is not necessarily a sign of a healthy, balanced, or sustainable economy. In small, specialised jurisdictions like the Crown Dependencies it can equally reflect high sectoral concentration (a single industry dominating output), volatility (large swings driven by a handful of firms or regulatory changes), and divergence between what is produced on the island and what residents actually earn. An economy can record rising per-head output while median household incomes stagnate, inequality widens, or the tax base narrows. It is also worth noting that international pressure on tax-advantaged business models is a structural risk to the revenues that underpin much of this output — one that does not show up in these numbers.

Concept differences

The Isle of Man publishes GDP using the income method. Jersey publishes GVA at basic prices (excluding taxes on products). Guernsey publishes GDP at market prices. UK figures are ONS GVA at basic prices (ABML ÷ EBAQ). These are related but distinct measures; the gap is small for island economies but series are not strictly comparable.

Calendar year alignment

IoM data covers financial years (April–March). The IoM figure for FY 20XX/XX is compared against the calendar year 20XX for UK, Jersey, and Guernsey — following the convention used in IoM National Income reports.

IoM methodology break (2007)

IoM nominal GDP per capita has a break at 2007/08 when ESA10 (European System of Accounts 2010) was adopted. The pre-2007 series (Digest of Economic and Social Statistics 2009, Table 15.5) uses factor-cost methodology. The post-2007 series uses ESA10 market-prices basis. The ratio of IoM to UK jumps from 116% (2006/07) to 167% (2007/08), reflecting both genuine finance-sector growth and the methodology change. These two segments must not be read as a continuous series.

IoM post-2012 per capita

Real series deflators

Each jurisdiction uses a different deflator — all expressed in approximate 2024 prices:

  • Isle of Man: nominal GDP pc deflated by IoM CPI (Statistics IoM, Jan 2008=100, rebased to 2024-25=100). CPI ≠ GDP deflator; IoM does not publish a GDP deflator. 2008/09–2023/24.
  • Jersey: Statistics Jersey constant 2024 price series (GVA pc). Own deflator implicit in published volume estimates. 2001–2024.
  • Guernsey: Statistics Guernsey constant 2023 price series (GDP pc). Own deflator implicit. 2009–2023. One year offset from 2024 base — levels slightly understated relative to Jersey and UK.
  • UK: implicit GVA deflator derived as ABML (nominal GVA) ÷ ABMI (chained volume GVA, ONS real-time database latest vintage), rebased to 2024=100. 1955–2024.

Comparability warning

These real series are directionally comparable but not precisely equivalent. CPI-deflated IoM figures will differ from GDP-deflator-deflated figures, particularly in years with large housing cost movements. Cross-jurisdiction level comparisons in real terms should be treated as indicative. For rigorous comparison of purchasing power levels, the nominal per-capita tab is more transparent about what is and is not being measured.

Annual percentage change in real GDP or GVA — the cleanest cross-jurisdictional comparison because it abstracts from both price-level differences and deflator methodology. The Isle of Man ran extraordinarily hot through the late 1990s and 2000s (eGaming, insurance, and finance expansion), with growth rates exceeding 5% most years before the GFC. The pattern since 2015 is more mixed: a 7.4% spike in 2016/17 driven by eGaming and corporate profits, followed by stagnation and two consecutive years of contraction (−5.0% in 2022/23, −2.5% in 2023/24). Jersey contracted almost every year from 2001–2013 in real terms — a prolonged post-dotcom and post-GFC squeeze largely unreported in nominal figures — before recovering strongly from 2014, including a remarkable +10.6% in 2021. Guernsey and the UK track each other broadly, though Guernsey is more volatile and recorded −2.4% in 2023.

Note on comparability

Real growth rates are more comparable across jurisdictions than nominal levels, as they abstract from both price differences and exchange rates. However, the underlying deflators differ (IoM uses its own CPI; Jersey uses Jersey RPI; Guernsey uses Guernsey RPI; UK uses GDP deflator). Growth rates should be treated as directionally comparable, not precisely equivalent.

This chart asks a simple question: how does each island's output per person compare to the UK? A reading of 100% means parity — the island produces the same per head as the UK average. Above 100% means more; below means less. Jersey has sat between 170–200% of UK for the entire period shown — roughly double — reflecting its position as a major international financial centre. Guernsey runs similarly high. The Isle of Man crossed UK parity around 2005/06 and is now estimated at roughly 190% of UK, though the 2016/17 peak has not been regained in real terms. The jump in 2007 is partly a measurement change, not purely economic growth.

Construction. Ratio = jurisdiction nominal GDP or GVA per head ÷ UK GVA per head (ONS ABML÷EBAQ), expressed as a percentage. IoM pre-2007: factor-cost GDP per head (Digest 2009, Table 15.5) using the same ONS UK denominator series; the ratio was 86% in 1997/98, reaching 116% in 2006/07. IoM 2007–2012: ESA10 GDP per head from NI Accounts 2012/13 Table 4, using the UK comparator as published therein (ONS ABML÷EBAQ, 2013 Blue Book vintage); ratio 167% in 2007/08, 217% in 2012/13. IoM 2013–2023: derived nominal GDP pc ÷ ABML÷EBAQ (current vintage); ratio approximate given population interpolation uncertainty. Jersey: GVA (basic) pc ÷ UK GVA pc. Guernsey: GDP (market prices) pc ÷ UK GVA pc — Guernsey's numerator includes taxes on products, UK's denominator excludes them, introducing a small upward bias (~2–3pp).

The 2007/08 IoM discontinuity reflects ESA10 adoption: FISIM reclassification, R&D capitalisation (adding ~1.5–2% to GDP), revised NPISH treatment, and alignment to European accounts standards. IoM Treasury estimated the modernised 2007/08 GDP at £2.84bn vs the original £2.03bn — a 40% uplift from methodology alone. The pre- and post-2007 sub-series are shown in distinct line styles; they should not be interpolated.

What this shows

Nominal GDP or GVA per head for each Crown Dependency expressed as a percentage of UK GVA per head (ONS ABML ÷ EBAQ). A ratio above 100% means the island economy's per-head output exceeds the UK average.

IoM methodology break

The IoM series has a hard break at 2007/08 (ESA10 adoption). Pre-2007 ratios use factor-cost GDP (Digest 2009 Table 15.5) against the same UK GVA denominator — the jump from 116% to 167% is partly genuine growth, partly methodology. The two segments are shown in distinct styles.

Construction. Each series is indexed to 100 at the selected base year. Divergence above or below 100 reflects cumulative real growth or contraction relative to that anchor. The chart shows volume change, not absolute levels — a jurisdiction at 120 has grown 20% in real terms since the base year, regardless of whether it was richer or poorer than another at the start.

Series construction by jurisdiction: IoM — built from chain-linked annual growth rates (IoM NI reports, Table 3), anchored to 100 at the base year and compounded forward and backward. This propagates any measurement error in individual growth-rate observations; accumulated error over 10+ years may be material. Jersey — Statistics Jersey constant 2024 price GVA series, rescaled to base year = 100; level-based, no chaining error. Guernsey — Statistics Guernsey constant 2023 price GDP per head series, rescaled to base year = 100; available from 2009 only, so base years before 2009 require backward extrapolation using growth rates, introducing additional uncertainty. UK — ONS ABML÷ABMI implicit GVA deflator applied to nominal per-head series, rebased to base year = 100; covers 1955–2025.

Base year choice. The default base year of 2009 corresponds to the post-GFC output trough — a natural common anchor from which diverging recovery trajectories are analytically informative. It is also the first year for which all four real series have data. Base years before 2009 exclude Guernsey; base years before 2001 exclude Jersey's real series. Avoid anchoring at years with known outliers (e.g. 2020) unless specifically examining COVID recovery, as this creates confounded index values in flanking years.

These indices should be read as approximate cumulative volume change, not precise percentage differences.
