Every life matters. This is about how suicide statistics are calculated and read, not whether the issue is serious. It is. It uses only population-level figures, no individual is identifiable, and it follows Samaritans media guidelines throughout. If you or someone you know is struggling, the support lines are at the foot of this page.

The islands don't have a suicide problem.
They have a data problem.

Sometimes I hate statistics. I mean, really hate statistics. These last few years, the Isle of Man has been told its suicide rate is soaring: "highest in a decade," "the only place in the British Isles to rise in the pandemic." It made me wonder. Does the Isle of Man have a problem, or is it an Island problem? So I started a study, and looked at all the islands. And I immediately found a pattern.

Act IThe pattern, across every island

The question stopped being an Isle of Man question. It became one that took in all the islands I looked at. Plotted as the official 3-year rolling age-standardised rate, they tower over a flat England & Wales. Guernsey more than doubles, to 15. The Isle of Man climbs to nearly 18, well above England & Wales near 11. It looks like an emergency, and it looks like the same emergency in every place at once.

A dozen different places. Different economies, different geographies, hundreds of miles apart. All apparently in crisis, all at the same time. That was the pattern. The question was why.

Fourteen years, four jurisdictions, the same recurring themes: small close-knit communities, isolation, service access, stigma, the volatility of small numbers. Official agencies caution against over-reading single-year rates every time. The alarm recurs anyway. That persistence is the tell.

Act IIISo I ran the numbers properly

Before I could talk myself into an answer, I wrote the study down and registered it publicly (OSF), so the method was fixed before I saw the result. The registered hypothesis was plain. Do the small island jurisdictions have genuinely higher suicide rates than Great Britain, and if so, why?

We built a panel of territories from 2001 to 2024: the small islands, with England & Wales and Scotland as large-population anchors. We benchmarked correctly, because there is no single "UK" rate to beat. England & Wales sits near 11, Scotland near 14. Then we tested it several ways: a fixed-effects Poisson model with a wild cluster bootstrap; empirical-Bayes shrinkage; the pre-registered panel; a synthetic control for Shetland with placebo inference; and a year-by-year analysis of the Isle of Man. We cross-checked the whole thing in a second statistical engine (R).

Here is what stopped us, and it cuts both ways. No island could be shown to differ from its comparator. The pre-registered panel returned a rate ratio of 1.004, a clean null. But the deeper finding is the one that matters: at these numbers, the rate has almost no power to detect even a large difference. The pooled result looks decisive only because England & Wales carries it. The small islands, on their own, cannot demonstrate a difference of any plausible size, in either direction. The rate is not telling us the islands are fine. It is telling us it cannot answer the question at all.

A note on Guernsey. It publishes only 3-year smoothed counts, so it could not enter the pre-registered panel at all; its verdict here rests on the direct comparison of rates and confidence intervals. That it is the hardest island to test in the first place is the data problem in miniature.

Public Health had told the committee much of this: that at these numbers the rate cannot demonstrate a statistically significant difference, and that it is "not very sensitive."

"A suicide rate is still … a rare event. So in order to demonstrate a statistically significant difference, whether up or down, it needs a long time … it is very unlikely that we would say there is a statistically significant change either up or down in that short period."Public Health evidence, SAPRC First Report 2025-26 (PP 2026/0133), 684 pages

But they defended the 3-year average and stopped there. They did not take the next step: that the apparent elevation is an artefact of that very average, and that the underlying rate sits at the England & Wales level. That step is what this analysis adds. Which leaves one obvious question. If there is no real difference, why does every island look like it is on fire?

Act IVOne year, not a trend

How the machine works. The headline is a 3-year or 5-year rolling average, the smoothing that official agencies (ONS among them) recommend for small populations, to damp volatility and protect identities. But put one extraordinary year through that smoother and it does not stay one year. 2020 was genuinely hard: the pandemic, and for the Isle of Man a backlog of inquests all concluding at once. That single spike is then spread across every overlapping window, plotted a year or two early, and kept in the figures long after the real peak has passed. On tiny counts, that one design choice is the whole difference between "ordinary" and "crisis," and it does it to every island at once.

Strip the rolling average away and look at the Isle of Man's actual annual registrations on a plain linear scale. The "crisis" is one year, 2020, and even that is inflated: 24 registered, but 19 actually occurred. The rest is inquest timing.

Excluding 2020, the Island averages about 11.6 per 100,000, effectively the England & Wales level. As 2020 leaves the rolling window, the "record" rate falls back.

A dozen places moving together is not a dozen separate local crises. It is two things they share: one hard year, and the same smoothing that stretches it into a decade. (Shetland is the exception that proves it. Its rate genuinely fell after 2014, and even that cannot be told apart from noise at this scale.)

Bad statistics are dangerous.
They frighten communities, stigmatise islands, and send scarce help chasing a problem that may not be there, while every real death still matters just as much.
Act VThe conclusion
So, do the islands have a suicide problem?
No, not one the data can show. They have a data problem.
Not "proven identical": the numbers are too sparse to rule out a modest difference either way. Which is the point. The rate cannot answer the question it is being asked. And this is not "small numbers, so it does not matter." Every death warrants review, exactly as Public Health argues. It means the rate should inform, not alarm.
The value of a clear answer

A 684-page committee report reached the caveat but not the conclusion. This analysis reached it in a day, pro bono, pre-registered, cross-checked in two independent statistical engines. One cost the taxpayer a great deal to arrive nowhere in particular. The other, freely, may have spared a little money and a great deal of fear.

In Canada. In Scotland. In Jersey. In Guernsey. And here at home.

Policy suggestions: what to do instead
Redirect the effort to action
Stop explaining a difference that isn't there
Stop hunting a special island cause
"Why is our rate higher?" cannot be answered from the rate, not for want of effort, but because the numbers cannot carry it. The inconclusive committee search is not a failure. It is the correct answer, arriving. Put that effort into case-level review of every death, and into the actions below.
Formalises the Committee's own evidence
Monitor what's sensitive, not what's blunt
Public Health itself asked for richer measures than the headline rate: a "suicide prevention profile," and the reach of interventions (people trained, people supported, waits shortened). Track those. They change in months, not the decade the rate needs.
Public Health's own ask
Clear leadership and delivery
The committee found "frustration with the pace of change, a lack of clear leadership for the Strategy, and an inability to work with the third sector." Name an accountable owner. Work with the charities that are already doing the work.
Governance
Do what works, whether or not any island differs
Prevention that stands on its own
Measure labour-market exclusion properly, then act on it
It is the strongest risk signal in the whole picture. The Island's own audit (GD2023/0019) found a third of those who died were unemployed or unable to work, a 4.1x overrepresentation. Yet across the islands it is measured inconsistently, on different bases (JSA here, claimant count there, different denominators everywhere), so it cannot even be compared. Measure it on a common basis, publish it beside the mortality data, and treat employment and financial security as frontline suicide prevention.
GD2023/0019 · 4.1xStrongest known signal
Fix the two-year mental-health waiting list
The single most-repeated grievance across 684 pages, and the reason ManxPACT exists. No data reform matters more than a two-year wait for adult mental-health support.
Access first
Back the peer support that already works, as a bridge, not a substitute
The Island already has award-winning peer support, Ed Space's men's group among it, and adult mental-health services refer into it. That matters: men, and the economically excluded, carry most of the risk and are hardest to reach through clinical routes. Resource and formalise that kind of community support as a genuine bridge while people wait, alongside treatment, never as a way to move them off the list instead of treating them. ManxPACT proposed exactly this and was turned down.
Award-winning, local, low-cost
Adopt BS 30480, the workplace suicide standard
The UK's first workplace suicide standard (BSI, 2025) is free and adoptable across Government and major employers. Given the GD2023/0019 unemployment finding above, it is a direct and immediate local mandate.
Free, off-the-shelfGD2023/0019
Fix the data
So the number stops manufacturing crises
A published, occurrence-based database, on the Scotland (ScotSID) model
The Island already runs a real-time suspected-suicide surveillance tool (Strategy objective 2.3.2). Evolve it into a transparent, occurrence-based, comparable record. Figures published as standard, not prised out by FOI.
Builds on existing workScotSID precedent
Publish annual counts. Retire the rolling headline
National Records of Scotland already publishes annual council-area counts, including years of 0, 1 or 2. Honest annual counts, presented with care, are more transparent than 3-year smoothing, not less safe. The rolling-average headline is what turns one year into a trend.
Scotland already does this
Harmonise measurement across the islands
Crown Dependencies use a 3-year window, the Scottish islands a 5-year one, England & Wales an annual one. Some count by registration, others differ (see the two charts above, they are not even on the same ruler). A common basis is needed before any cross-island comparison means anything.
