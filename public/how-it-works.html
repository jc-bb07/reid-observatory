How the power gets made

Wind, sun, atoms, water, tide, chemistry and gas. Each one makes electricity in its own way, and each one has to be tamed so the lights stay steady. Play with them below.

Two ways to make AC power

Every generator on this page belongs to one of two families. Which family it's in decides how it behaves when something goes wrong on the grid, and that matters more than the fuel.

Spinning machines

  • A magnet turns inside coils of copper and the wave comes out naturally. Gas, nuclear, hydro and older wind work this way.
  • Inertia comes free. Tonnes of spinning steel resist any change in speed, so a sudden loss of supply slows the grid gently instead of crashing it.
  • Big fault current. In a short circuit they dump several times their rating, which is exactly what tells protection relays to trip.

Inverters

  • Solar panels and batteries make DC. Electronics switch it thousands of times a second to sculpt an AC wave. Modern wind turbines also sit behind inverters.
  • No natural inertia, and limited fault current because the silicon would cook. The fix is software: a grid-forming inverter mimics a spinning machine on purpose.
  • Grids with mostly inverters already run on islands like Kauai and the Faroes. It works when the control rules are written for it.

Wind

Blades are wings. Wind flowing over them creates lift that turns a rotor, a gearbox or direct-drive generator turns that into electricity, and a power converter tidies it up for the grid. Power rises with the cube of wind speed: double the wind, eight times the power.

How it's controlled

  • Yaw: motors turn the whole nacelle to face the wind.
  • Below rated speed the controller adjusts rotor speed to squeeze out every last watt, a trick called maximum power point tracking.
  • Above rated speed the blades twist (pitch) to spill wind and hold output flat. It's the same idea as feathering a propeller.
  • Storms: at about 25 m/s the blades feather fully and the rotor parks. Newer machines ramp down gently instead of stopping dead.
  • On command: the grid operator can cap output (curtailment), ask for headroom so the turbine can push up if frequency drops, and require it to ride through faults instead of tripping off.
On the Island. MUA's onshore scheme, Cair Vie at Earystane and Scard, plans five turbines producing about 130 GWh a year, around a third of demand; it was still at pre-application in July 2026. Offshore, Ørsted's Mooir Vannin proposes 87 turbines and 1,400 MW, mostly for export, with up to 100 MW that could be supplied to the Island. Its examination closed in September 2026 and the decision now sits with the Council of Ministers.

Who sets the rules: turbine design to the IEC 61400 standards; behaviour on the grid set by the network operator's connection requirements (in Great Britain, the Grid Code and ENA G99).

Solar

A solar cell is a sandwich of two kinds of silicon. Light knocks electrons loose and the junction between the layers pushes them one way round a circuit. No moving parts at all. The catch at 54° north is obvious: output follows the sun, the season and the clouds.

How it's controlled

  • The inverter is the brain. Panels can run at any voltage; only one gives maximum power. The inverter hunts for it constantly as light changes: that's the dot on the curve.
  • Anti-islanding. If the grid goes dead, the inverter must switch off within a fraction of a second, so it never back-feeds lines that engineers think are safe.
  • Voltage support. Modern inverters can soak up or push out reactive power to steady the local voltage, and ease off output if the street's voltage climbs on a sunny day.
  • Hot is bad. Panels lose roughly 0.3 to 0.4% of output per degree above 25°C, so a cold bright March day beats a sticky July one.
On the Island. Around a thousand homes and businesses already have rooftop solar. MUA asked landowners in February 2026 to offer sites for ground-mounted arrays, and a 735 kW roof at the National Sports Centre has approval. In winter the sun barely clears the hills, which is why solar here pairs with imports and storage.

Who sets the rules: connection standards for small generators (in Great Britain, ENA G98 and G99) fix how fast an inverter disconnects and how it behaves on voltage and frequency. MUA sets the requirements for its own network.

Nuclear

Split a uranium-235 atom with a slow neutron and it releases heat plus two or three new neutrons. If, on average, exactly one of those goes on to split another atom, the reaction holds steady. The heat boils water, the steam spins a turbine. Everything about control is managing that "exactly one".

How it's controlled

  • Control rods of boron, cadmium or hafnium soak up neutrons. Push them in and k drops below 1; pull them out and power rises.
  • Delayed neutrons make it controllable. A small fraction of neutrons arrive seconds late, which slows the whole reaction down to a pace engineers and machines can manage.
  • Built-in brake. In a water-cooled reactor, hotter water slows fewer neutrons, so rising temperature cuts power by itself. Try pulling the rods: power climbs, then settles at a new, hotter level.
  • Scram drops every rod in seconds. Fission stops, but decay heat carries on at around 6% of full power and fades over hours and days. Losing the cooling, not the chain reaction, is what caused Fukushima.
  • Steady rather than nimble. Large reactors run flat out; output can be varied, but slowly. Small modular reactors aim to be factory-built and smaller, though none yet runs in the UK.
On the Island. There is no nuclear plant on the Island and no plan for one. Nuclear reaches us indirectly: Britain's grid, which supplies the interconnector, includes stations such as Heysham across the bay in Lancashire.

Who sets the rules: in the UK, the Office for Nuclear Regulation licenses sites and approves designs (via Generic Design Assessment), with the environment agencies covering discharges and waste; internationally, IAEA safety standards.

Hydro

Water held high has stored energy. Let it fall through a turbine and you get power in proportion to two things: how much water flows, and how far it drops. It is the original grid regulator, because a gate can open in seconds.

0head (drop), m
0output, kW
Power ≈ water density × gravity × flow × head × efficiency (about 88%). Small numbers here: Sulby-sized scheme.

How it's controlled

  • The governor opens or closes the gate to hold the generator's speed, and so the grid's frequency, steady. Hydro plants were the first automatic frequency regulators.
  • Pumped storage runs the same machine backwards: pump water uphill when power is cheap, let it down when it's needed. Dinorwig in Wales goes from standstill to full output in well under a minute.
  • Limits are wet ones: rainfall, reservoir safety, and the water the river needs downstream for fish and for drinking supply.
On the Island. Sulby's hydro plant is tiny by grid standards: its record year, 2012 to 2013, produced 4.9 GWh, about 1% of demand. The reservoirs' first job is drinking water, which caps how much power they can ever give.

Who sets the rules: reservoir and dam safety law, abstraction and river-flow rules, plus the same grid connection requirements as any generator.

Tidal

The moon drags the sea back and forth twice a day. Put an underwater turbine where the current runs fast and you can harvest it. Seawater is about 800 times denser than air, so slow currents carry serious power. Its great virtue: you can print the timetable decades ahead.

0current, m/s
0output, MW (1.5 MW unit)

How it's controlled

  • Predictable, not dispatchable. Output peaks four times a day as the flood and ebb run hardest, then falls to zero at slack water. Those peaks march about 50 minutes later each day, so they rarely line up with teatime demand.
  • Turbines either swing round or flip their blades when the tide reverses, and pitch or speed control caps output in the fastest flows, just like wind.
  • Spring tides, around new and full moon, carry far more power than neaps, because power climbs with the cube of current speed.
On the Island. The Irish Sea has strong tidal streams in places, and studies around 2015 looked at Manx sites, but nothing has progressed since. Treat tidal as a watching brief, not part of any current plan.

Who sets the rules: marine licensing and seabed leasing (on the Island, through government and the Crown's marine estate), environmental assessment, and the usual grid connection requirements.

Batteries

A battery doesn't make energy, it moves it in time. Its superpower on a grid is speed: it can go from charging to full discharge in a fraction of a second, faster than any spinning machine can react. Whether it helps or just follows depends on how its inverter is programmed.

-outcome

How it's controlled

  • Grid-following batteries measure the grid's frequency, then react. That measuring takes a few hundred milliseconds, by which time frequency has already fallen a long way.
  • Grid-forming batteries hold their own voltage wave steady, like a spinning generator does, so they push back instantly. That's synthetic inertia, and it's the key to running a grid without big spinning plant.
  • Hours, not days. Grid batteries usually store one to four hours. Brilliant for shocks and evening peaks; useless for a windless fortnight in January.
  • Under-frequency load shedding is the last line of defence: if frequency falls far enough, relays automatically switch off blocks of customers to save the rest.
On the Island. There is no grid-scale battery yet. The Faroe Islands show what a modest one can do: a 6 MW battery roughly doubled the useful output of one wind farm. Powering Mann argues for 30 to 40 MW of grid-forming storage here.

Who sets the rules: grid codes are being rewritten to define grid-forming behaviour (Great Britain introduced a grid-forming specification in 2022), plus fire-safety and siting standards for large lithium installations.

Gas, for comparison

Pulrose is a combined-cycle gas turbine: a jet engine bolted to the ground. It burns gas to spin one generator, then catches the hot exhaust to boil water and spin a second. That second trick takes efficiency from roughly a third to well over half.

How it's controlled

  • Governor: fuel valves open and close to hold speed at exactly 3,000 rpm for a two-pole machine, which is 50 Hz. Lose the cable and the governor opens up to fill the gap.
  • Exciter: varies the magnetic field in the generator to hold voltage and supply reactive power.
  • Flexible but thirsty for money: it can follow demand up and down within limits, but every unit costs fuel bought on a volatile market, and burns carbon.
On the Island. Pulrose's combined-cycle unit, about 85 MW and running since 2003, made roughly 82% of Manx electricity at the end of 2024. Its design life runs out in the first half of the 2030s, which is why the next decade's choices matter so much. The Richmond Hill energy-from-waste plant adds about 10%.

Who sets the rules: emissions permits, gas safety regulation, and on the grid, the operator's dispatch instructions.

Words worth knowing

MW and MWh
A megawatt is a rate, like speed. A megawatt-hour is an amount, like distance. A 3 MW turbine running flat out for an hour makes 3 MWh.
Frequency (50 Hz)
How fast the grid's AC wave cycles. It sags when demand exceeds supply and rises when supply exceeds demand, so it's the grid's live balance meter.
Inertia
The stored spin in big rotating machines that slows down any change in frequency, buying time for controls to act.
Grid-forming
An inverter that sets its own voltage wave and resists change like a spinning machine, instead of merely following the grid.
Curtailment
Telling a wind or solar plant to produce less than it could, usually because the network can't take or use the power.
Capacity factor
Actual yearly output divided by what the plant would make at full power all year.
Scram
Emergency shutdown of a reactor by inserting all control rods.
Anti-islanding
The rule that small generators must shut off when the grid goes dead, to protect line workers.
