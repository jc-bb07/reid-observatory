How concrete is the policy?①

Word share says how much space a candidate gave a subject. It says nothing about whether they committed to anything. So every policy statement in both manifestos was read and classified: does it name a mechanism, number or deadline (Concrete), promise a review or consultation that defers the actual decision (Process), or state a belief or priority with nothing defined behind it (Values)? Where does each candidate commit, and where do they keep options open? Across the five shared themes, James's manifesto is 68% concrete against Jane's 11%, and 43% of Jane's content sets out a priority or belief without a defined mechanism behind it — a legitimate, common way to write a manifesto, especially for an incumbent minister keeping room to negotiate within Government. It isn't even across her platform, though: Education leans on process rather than open-ended aspiration — five separate "a review of…" commitments — and she names two genuinely structural moves (a single patient record; zero-based budgeting). Health & care is where her platform stays most open-ended: 60% priority-setting language ("effective and timely," "better integration," "improved access") without a named lever.

Five themes, side by side

Where both candidates cover the same ground, this is what they're actually proposing — not just how much space they gave it.

Economy

James Cochrane

Diagnosis first: the Island's output has fallen against the UK's since its 2019/20 peak, because growth leaned on one sector (e-gaming) while it pushed up costs everywhere else. Wants government to actively regulate margins that stay "stubbornly high" and renegotiate the Sea Services Agreement to cut freight and travel costs.

Jane Poole-Wilson

Continuity first: unemployment is at 0.7%, and the priority is preparing for the Island's Moneyval assessment while keeping tax policy competitive for inward investment. Government's role is partnership with the private sector and closing skills gaps, not intervening in pricing.

The contrast: James wants government to actively diagnose and intervene on concentration and cost; Jane wants government to hold the current settlement steady and support the sectors already here.

Housing

James Cochrane

Specific levers: starter homes for first-time buyers, a self-build scheme tied to taking on apprentices, help-to-buy sized for two incomes, and a compulsory landlord register — the 2021 Act made it law, but it's still voluntary five years on. Wants the 5,695 vacant homes (13% of stock) accounted for publicly.

Jane Poole-Wilson

Framework first: build on the 2024 Objective Assessment of Housing Need so the next Strategic Plan weighs housing type, not just numbers; incentives (not compulsion) to bring empty properties back into use; one Minister given cross-department responsibility to drive delivery.

The contrast: James proposes numeric targets and one compulsory rule; Jane proposes a stronger planning framework and monitoring, with no numeric targets attached.

Government & finance

James Cochrane

Structural fixes with numbers attached: one governance rulebook for all departments, Chief Officers legally answerable for their finances, a hiring freeze on back-office roles and the reinstated 2015 headcount cap, government accounts to IFRS or GAAP standard. His one deferral is asking for an "evidence-led analysis of the options" before Tynwald decides — the exception, not the rule, in this theme.

Jane Poole-Wilson

Eleven-point programme, almost entirely process: a strategic-priorities programme, periodic service-design reviews, "improved data and management information," "engaging relevant expertise." The one named mechanism is zero-based budgeting — everything else is a commitment to plan, monitor or review rather than to change something specific now.

The contrast: this is James's strongest theme for named, checkable change (5 concrete items of 7) and Jane's most process-heavy (7 of 11 items promise a review, plan or report rather than a decision).

Education

James Cochrane

Named and dated: mandatory PSHE/RSHE with a defined opt-out, every child into a club or activity aged 11–16, family-law cases resolved within 26 weeks with shared care the default, a funded Care Certificate route into care work, and finishing the long-stalled SEN Bill into statute with waiting times published.

Jane Poole-Wilson

Five separate "a review of…" commitments — qualification pathways from 14+, 6th-form provision, apprenticeship models, a technology-impact forecast, a public consultation on online harms — plus continuing the existing phone-restriction policy. Real commitments to look, not yet to change anything specific.

The contrast: this is the theme where Jane is process-driven rather than values-only — she's promising to review and consult, not just to hope. Still nothing dated or numbered the way James's proposals are.

Health & care

James Cochrane

Implement, don't review: ten independent reviews since 2019 are enough evidence. His numbers say the hospital's bottleneck is discharge, not beds — 55 patients medically fit to leave but stuck — and he'd fund the fix there before adding capacity. Specific asks: bowel screening from 50, and a funding fix for the Hospice (Government covers 24% of its income against Jersey's 44%).

Jane Poole-Wilson

Systems and process: acknowledges long waits and fragmented records, and wants a single patient record and better handover between providers. On funding sustainability, she'd convene a Citizens' Assembly to build public agreement on options, rather than naming one herself.

The contrast: James's health section is 80% concrete (named ages, percentages, benchmarks); Jane's is 60% values language — "effective," "improved," "better integration" — with a single patient record and text/email communication her only two named mechanisms.

▼ Data governance & methodology notes
① Policy-specificity methodology

Both manifestos were converted from PDF to text and read section by section. Every discrete policy statement in the five themes both candidates cover (Economy, Housing, Government & finance, Education, Health & care) was classified into one of three buckets: Concrete — names a mechanism, number, threshold or deadline; Process — commits to a review, consultation, plan or report that defers the actual decision; Values — states a belief, priority or aspiration with nothing defined behind it. 88 statements were classified in total (James: 41, Jane: 47). This is one person's manual read of prose, not a mechanical word count — a small number of statements sit genuinely on a boundary (for example, "maintaining the policy on restrictions on mobile phones" was scored Concrete as a specific, checkable rule; "continued investment in community care" was scored Values as an undefined commitment). Re-classification by a different reader would move individual items but is unlikely to reverse the overall pattern.

② Leadership-signal methodology

Counts are regex matches against the plain-text manifestos: numeric figures (£ amounts, percentages, and standalone multi-digit numbers), the phrases "I believe" and "I would support" / "I support", and the action verbs stop / fix / implement / build / cap / freeze. Named pledges are James's own explicit checklist ("The things you can hold me to"); Jane's manifesto contains no equivalent numbered list. This reflects one campaign document per candidate, not their voting record, staff, or governing style in office — it measures how each chose to write a manifesto, which is suggestive but not conclusive.

Sample scope

This page covers 1 of 12 constituencies (Middle) in full narrative depth. Figures here are not yet cross-checked against the other 11 seats — a Middle-specific pattern may or may not hold once all 24 candidates are measured the same way. Jane Poole-Wilson's manifesto also covers safety/migration and environment/energy, which James Cochrane's doesn't name as a section — read less as an omission than a division of labour: his platform runs on economics end to end, while safety and policing sit inside the portfolio she already held as Minister for Justice & Home Affairs. Worth checking whether that pattern — challengers campaigning on economics, incumbents campaigning on their own brief — holds across the other 23 candidates.
