Keep the Island at 50 Hz

Every second of every day, the electricity made on and imported to the Island must match what 85,000 people are using. Get it wrong and the frequency drifts; get it badly wrong and the lights go out. You're in the control room for one winter's day. Good luck.

The arithmetic that matters. The Island uses about 360 GWh a year. Demand swings from roughly 23 MW on a summer night to 75 MW on a cold winter evening. Pulrose and the cable carry nearly all of it, and both reach the end of their design lives in the early-to-mid 2030s.

2

MW is the tap, MWh is the bath

Power (watts) is how hard you're pulling right now. Energy (watt-hours) is how much you've pulled over time. Grids are built for the first and billed for the second, and mixing them up causes half of all energy arguments.

0power right now, kW
0energy today, kWh
£0cost today at 29.5p

A kettle is a big tap, briefly. 3 kW for four minutes is only 0.2 kWh. A heat pump is a small tap left running: perhaps 1 to 2 kW, but for hours.

Why the tap matters to the grid. Cables, transformers and power stations are sized for the moment everyone turns on at once. That's peak power, in MW. The bill mostly pays for energy, in MWh. A policy that shaves the peak can save more than one that saves the same energy at 3am.

Island scale. 360 GWh a year averages about 40 MW. The winter peak is nearly double that. The cable and Pulrose have to cover the peak, not the average.
3

The cable, and why it needs reactors

A long AC cable under the sea behaves like a giant capacitor. Before it carries a single useful watt, it soaks up current just charging and discharging fifty times a second. Big coils called shunt reactors at the ends cancel that out. Make the cable longer and the problem grows until AC stops making sense.

0charging, MVAr
0left for real power, MW

Reactive power is the froth on the pint. It takes up room in the glass (the cable's current rating) without quenching anyone's thirst. Reactors skim the froth so there's room for beer.

Lose a reactor and the operator has to manage high voltage some other way, often by limiting how much the cable carries or running more gas at Pulrose. That costs money every hour it lasts.

HVDC has no charging current at all, so it works over hundreds of kilometres. The price is converter stations at each end, which are expensive and long-lead. That's the trade-off behind any second link.

Illustrative physics: assumes a cable capacitance of about 0.2 µF per km and a 60 MW thermal rating at 90 kV, scaled with voltage.

4

N-1: lose the biggest thing, keep the lights on

Grids are planned so that losing any single piece of kit, at the worst moment, doesn't black out customers. Engineers call it N-1. Switch things off below and see whether the Island survives a cold-snap peak.

Today the Island passes, thanks to old diesels. Lose the cable on a cold evening and Pulrose plus the diesels can cover it. Lose Pulrose and the cable plus diesels can too. That's why the diesel stations, some of them decades old, are still kept fuelled.

Now try 2035 with nothing replaced. Pulrose has retired and the cable is past its design life. Heat pumps and electric cars have pushed the peak up. Drag the demand slider and watch the margin vanish.

Two diverse links plus a battery is one way to pass. The point of the exercise isn't the answer; it's that the question has a deadline.

Cheapest first: the merit order

At any moment the system runs the cheapest source available first, then the next cheapest, until demand is met. On the Island that's mostly a choice between importing over the cable and burning gas at Pulrose. Move the prices and watch the stack reshuffle.

£0cost this hour

Waste and rooftop solar go first because, once built, their fuel is free. Then whichever is cheaper of the cable and Pulrose. Diesels are the expensive last resort.

The cable is a ceiling. When imports are cheap you'd happily take more, but the link can only carry so much. Everything above that line is gas, at whatever gas costs that day.

Hedging smooths the bumps. Gas is bought months ahead to stop one bad week landing on bills. When forward gas prices more than doubled in March 2026, Manx tariffs held because most of the year's gas was already bought.

Fuel cost assumes about 29.3 kWh per therm and 50% efficiency. Excludes carbon, maintenance and capital costs.

6

Heat pumps, cars and the 5.30pm problem

Moving heating and transport onto electricity is the biggest change coming to the grid. It's good news for the climate and for imported-fuel bills, but it lands on the winter evening peak unless we're clever about timing.

75winter peak, MW
360yearly use, GWh

Heat pumps run all day in a cold snap. Averaged across many homes, each adds perhaps 1.5 to 2 kW at peak. Good insulation cuts that sharply, which is why fabric comes first.

Cars are easier. Most sit still overnight. Plug in at 5.30pm and charge immediately, and you've added to the peak. Let a smart charger wait until after 11pm and the same energy costs the grid almost nothing.

Smart meters make this possible by letting tariffs reward off-peak use. The Island's rollout reached 27,500 of about 50,000 meters by August 2025, with completion targeted for the end of 2026.

Illustrative assumptions: 1.8 kW per heat pump at peak (1.0 kW if well insulated); unmanaged EVs add 0.7 kW each at peak, managed ones 0.1 kW; yearly use about 4,000 kWh per heat pump and 2,400 kWh per car.

Build a 2030 mix

Climate policy says Manx electricity should be carbon-neutral by 2030, and the Government's own progress report rates that "at risk". Try building a mix that gets there. Then face the hard test: a windless, grey week in January.

0%still from gas
0 MWfirm need, windless January evening

Energy over the year is the easy bit. Wind and solar can supply a big share of annual MWh. The hard bit is the evening in January when the wind drops and the sun has gone: something firm has to cover the peak.

Batteries help for hours, not days. They take the edge off the evening and steady the frequency, but they don't replace a cable or a power station for a fortnight-long calm.

Imports count if they're clean. More than a quarter of the electricity the Island imports is classed as zero-carbon. Whatever the cable can't bring has to be made here.

On the Island now. Proposals on the table include MUA's five-turbine onshore wind farm (about 130 GWh a year, not yet in planning), MUA's search for land for solar, and the offshore Mooir Vannin scheme, which is mainly for export and awaiting a decision. Who pays, and for how long

Grid kit lasts thirty to fifty years, so it's paid for like a mortgage: borrow now, repay through bills over decades. The interest rate and the repayment period matter as much as the price tag. Try it on a second interconnector.

Today's starting point. A typical Manx unit costs 29.5p plus a 25.5p daily standing charge. Jersey pays about 22p by importing most of its power. Manx Utilities is still repaying a £185m bond that runs to 2034.

Spread costs too thin and you pay twice. A longer loan lowers the yearly bill but raises the total. Too short, and today's bill payers fund assets their grandchildren will use.

Not doing it isn't free. Every option for the 2030s costs money; so does a winter of diesel if a single old cable fails. The comparison is always against the cost of the alternative, not against zero.

Simple annuity on about 38,000 households and 360 GWh a year. The £200m figure is Manx Utilities' preliminary estimate for a second interconnector (Tynwald answer, July 2026).

A Manx home, 2030

Same house, same family, same January. Scroll down and watch it change, one sensible upgrade at a time.

Meet the Kermodes

A 1990s semi, oil boiler in the back, teenagers who think doors close themselves. Heat leaks from the loft, the walls and the floor. The oil tank gets topped up at whatever the price is that week.

Exposed to: heating oil prices, every draught

Plug the leaks first

Loft topped up, cavity filled, insulation under the floor, draughts sealed. Nothing glamorous, nothing you can show the neighbours. It's the cheapest upgrade per degree of warmth, and it shrinks everything that comes after.

Heat demand down by a quarter or more

Swap the boiler for a heat pump

The oil tank goes. A heat pump pulls warmth from outside air even on a frosty night, delivering roughly three units of heat for every unit of electricity. Grants under Heat Wise Home cover a large part of the cost for eligible households.

Imported oil bill: gone

A smart meter and a smarter tariff

The house now knows what time it is. Cheaper power off-peak, dearer at 5.30pm. The heat pump pre-warms the house in the afternoon and coasts through teatime, so nobody notices except the bill.

Evening draw shifted out of the peak

Panels on the roof, a battery in the garage

Summer days fill the battery; it empties again at teatime. In January the panels do less, but the battery can still fill up overnight on cheap power and cover the peak instead.

The house leans on the grid least when the grid is busiest

The car waits its turn

Plugged in at six, charging from eleven. Full by breakfast, never touching the teatime peak. One day, the car may even lend the house some power back.

Same comfort. Lower bills. A grid that copes.

Five things people say about the grid

Tap a card to turn it over.

Words worth knowing

Frequency
The grid's heartbeat, 50 cycles a second. It falls when demand outruns supply and rises when supply outruns demand.
Interconnector
A cable linking two grids. The Island has one, a 104 km AC cable to Lancashire, in service since 2000.
Shunt reactor
A large coil that soaks up the excess "charging" current a long AC cable produces, keeping voltage under control.
N-1
The planning standard that the system must survive the loss of any single major component.
Merit order
Running the cheapest available source first, then the next, until demand is met.
Peak
The highest demand of the year, usually a cold weekday evening. It sets how much kit the Island needs.
Capacity factor
What a power source actually produces over a year, as a share of its maximum.
Time-of-use tariff
Electricity priced by the hour, so using power when the grid is quiet costs less.
