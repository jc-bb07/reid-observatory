The island's population reached 84,756 at the end of 2024 — a record high, though only just above the 2011 census of 84,497. Growth is slow and uneven. Between 2011 and 2016 the population actually fell by 1,183 as more people left than arrived. It has grown modestly since, but by less than 300 people a year on average.
Census anchors are confirmed Cabinet Office figures. Post-2021 annual estimates are experimental (Statistics IoM Population Report 2025, income tax proxy). The island's population is growing — but only because more people are arriving than leaving. Natural change (births minus deaths) subtracts around 330 people a year. Net migration adds around 600. Without migration, the island would be shrinking by roughly one person a day.

Government documents often cite the gross inward figure — around 2,200 people arriving each year. The relevant number for population change is net: around 600 after accounting for those who leave. That distinction matters.
Net migration 2022–2024: Statistics IoM Population Report 2025, Tables 3 and 7 (income tax proxy, experimental). Outward migration for 2024 is substantially estimated due to reporting lag. The accounting identity (pop change = natural change + net migration + residual) closes to within 20–50 people per year — consistent with data lag. 2017–2021 net migration: inter-censal residual divided equally across five years. This is not annual data — it is an average of the 2016–2021 inter-censal period, shown as a reference band, not as annual bars.

Net migration is not spread evenly across age groups. Working-age adults (25–64) account for nearly all of the net gain — around 660 people in 2024. Young adults aged 20–24 leave faster than they arrive: the island has no university, and this age group consistently emigrates to pursue education and early career opportunities elsewhere.

Notably, the over-65 net is close to zero. Older people both arrive and leave, roughly cancelling out. Migration is not driving the growth of the elderly population — that growth comes from existing residents ageing in place.

In 2021, 22.1% of the island's population — 18,569 people — were aged 65 or over. This is confirmed by the 2021 Census. The share has risen from 20.7% in 2016.

This growth is driven by existing residents ageing in place, not by migration. The net flow of people aged 65 and over from migration is close to zero. GP registration data, which tracks this stock annually, confirms the trend has continued since 2021.

The largest single age cohort in 2021 was the 50–59 year olds: 13,361 people. They will reach retirement age between 2027 and 2037. This is not a forecast — these people are already here. Their transition into the 65+ group is a demographic certainty, not a risk scenario.

An ageing population means greater demand on health and care services. The number of patients per GP has risen 45% since 2011 — from 1,851 to 2,689. Over the same period, the number of GPs has fallen from 47 to 33.

This is a compound pressure: more patients, and those patients are older. Older patients typically consult more frequently and have more complex needs. The average age at death has risen from 79 in 2017 to 81 in 2025 — people are living longer, meaning the elderly population accumulates faster than deaths clear it.
