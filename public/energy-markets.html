Who earns what heating your home

Three businesses compete to keep Manx homes warm: a government-owned electricity utility, a privately owned gas monopoly and a handful of oil distributors. We pulled their figures as far back as they're published and asked the same question of each: are they earning more than it costs them to raise the money?

Declaration. The author, James Cochrane MHK, has chaired Manx Utilities since October 2026, and a close family member holds a senior role in the UK business of the group that owns Ellan Vannin Fuels. Everything here is from public sources, and nothing draws on Manx Utilities board material. Read it with that in mind, and check the sources.

Prices, against the UK and Jersey

Manx electricity held below UK levels through the 2022 crisis, then caught up and overtook them. Manx gas spiked harder and faster than the UK cap, because it is bought forward and priced forward, and has stayed dearer since.

Heating oil: nobody's watching the margin

Oil heats a large share of Manx homes, has no price regulation, and the distributors publish no Island accounts. What we can see is the shelf price. Most of the time it tracks the UK closely; in the April 2026 spike it ran well ahead of Northern Ireland.

April 2026. One EVF customer's unit price went from 65.5p to 125.5p a litre, before VAT, between January and April. Manx Petroleum was quoting about 112p. Northern Ireland's average for 900 litres was about 101p.

June 2022. At the last big spike the Island was cheaper than Northern Ireland, about 91p against 103p. Island suppliers buy in shiploads, so prices lag the market both up and down.

The parent's returns. DCC, which owns EVF through Certas Energy, reports an 18.5% return on capital employed for its energy division, earning 3.52p of operating profit per litre across 15.2 billion litres. That's group-wide, not the Island, but it is the only audited figure in the chain.

Returns against the cost of capital

The Steam Packet test: is the business earning more on its assets than it costs to finance them? Persistent returns well above that line, in a market with no real competition, are the signature of supernormal profit.

Measures differ: return on fixed assets for Manx Utilities, return on capital employed (ROCE) for Manx Gas and DCC, return on assets for Jersey Electricity. Treat the ladder as orders of magnitude, not decimals. The dashed line is Manx Utilities' 2034 energy bond coupon, 5.375%.

Manx Utilities No excess

Operating returns of about 3 to 6.5% on assets in normal years, almost all paid straight out as roughly £20m a year in interest. Two crisis years ran deficits. Tynwald has written off £95m of its debt. It has the opposite problem: too little margin, too much debt.

Manx Gas Excess found

Targeted a 10.6% rolling ROCE, then agreed 9.99% from 2015, roughly double today's regulated 5.45% WACC. Since 2022 domestic tariffs are capped, but CURA found large-customer margins of about 44% and cut those tariffs in 2026. The company is taking legal advice.

Heating oil Can't tell

No Island accounts, no regulator and no margin data. The parent group's energy returns are high, and the Island price ran ahead in the 2026 spike. That is a reason to look, not proof. Only a market study with access to the books could answer it.

Jersey Electricity Comparator

A listed island utility earning a 7.4% return on assets in 2025 (6.4% over five years), paying dividends, with a lower unit rate than the Isle of Man. Proof that cheap island power and a positive return can coexist, largely thanks to cheap French imports.

Manx Utilities, the long run

Seventeen years of results. The headline surplus swings on one-offs: a £95m debt write-off in 2017/18, forward gas contracts revalued in 2021/22, the price crisis in 2022/23. Strip those out and the business has rarely made more than its interest bill.
